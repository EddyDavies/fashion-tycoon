# Prints & Patterns: Comprehensive Reference for Style Brief Builder

> **Purpose:** Reference document for the print/pattern selection layer of the style brief app. Covers print techniques, motif categories, placement terminology, colour complexity, artwork requirements, scale principles, and current trend context. Designed to inform UI options and copy, supplier matching logic, and brief-to-factory communication.

---

## 1. Print Technique Types

How the print is applied — the production method — determines cost, MOQ, fabric compatibility, and the visual character of the final garment.

---

### 1.1 Screen Printing

Screen printing uses a mesh screen (one per colour) to push ink through onto fabric. It remains the dominant method globally, accounting for roughly **65% of the worldwide printed fabric market**.

#### Manual Flat-Bed Screen Printing
- Screens are stretched over frames; a squeegee draws ink across the screen by hand.
- Best for: Small runs, artisanal or limited-edition pieces, heavy specialty inks (puff, glow, glitter).
- Colour limit: Practically 1–8 colours before complexity becomes unmanageable manually.
- Minimum order: Can be as low as 12–24 pieces per design/colour.
- Limitations: Labour-intensive; colour-to-colour registration less precise than rotary.

#### Rotary Screen Printing
- Perforated cylindrical nickel screens rotate continuously over moving fabric; ink is pumped from inside the drum through laser-engraved perforations.
- Speed: Up to 100 metres per minute; ideal for bulk orders from 200m+.
- Colour registration: Highly consistent — critical for large runs where uniformity matters.
- Specialty inks: Supports glitter, adhesives, sequins, pearlescent, metallic, and 3D particle inks.
- Resolution: Slightly lower fine detail than digital — complex photographic designs may need digital instead.
- Economic threshold: High setup cost per screen; only profitable above ~500–1,000m.
- **Canton Fair availability:** Widely available from fabric mills and garment manufacturers.

#### Discharge Printing
- A chemical agent (usually zinc formaldehyde sulfoxylate or sodium sulfoxylate) is printed onto pre-dyed fabric. When cured at 320–330°F, the agent destroys the existing dye in the printed area and can optionally replace it with a new pigment.
- Result: Ink sits below the fabric surface — extremely soft hand feel, vintage or faded aesthetic, no raised texture.
- Best fabrics: 100% cotton or natural fibres (bamboo, hemp). Cotton-polyester blends discharge partially — the polyester portion retains dye.
- Visual effects: Soft gradients, vintage fades, near-tie-dye effects without tie-and-dye labour.
- Limitations: Chemical handling requires proper ventilation; not suitable for synthetic fabrics.
- **Canton Fair availability:** Available from specialist screen printing suppliers; less common than standard plastisol/water-based screen printing.

---

### 1.2 Digital / DTG (Direct-to-Garment)

- Specialised inkjet printers apply water-based ink directly onto the finished garment surface.
- Unlimited colours and photographic-quality resolution; no screens required.
- Best for: Small-to-medium runs (1–500 pieces), designs with fine gradients or photographic detail, samples and prototypes.
- Best fabrics: 100% cotton or high-cotton blends (80%+). Polyester requires pre-treatment; results less vibrant.
- Dark garments require a white underbase layer, adding cost and slight stiffness.
- Wash durability: Good but typically lower than screen print plastisol if not properly cured.
- Limitations: Slow per-unit vs. screen printing at volume; not cost-competitive above ~300+ units for simple designs.
- **Canton Fair availability:** DTG machines are widely sold at the fair (DPES Expo at Canton Fair Complex); garment suppliers increasingly offer DTG as a low-MOQ custom service.

---

### 1.3 Sublimation / Dye Sublimation

- Ink is printed onto transfer paper in reverse, then heat-pressed onto fabric at ~180–200°C. The ink converts to gas and permanently bonds with the polymer fibres.
- Result: No raised feel, no cracking, no fading at seams — the ink becomes part of the fibre.
- **Fabric requirement:** Minimum 65% polyester content; 100% polyester gives the most vibrant result. Below 50% polyester, results are poor and wash out.
- On poly-cotton blends: ink only bonds to polyester fibres — cotton portion remains undyed, creating a "heathered" or vintage look (sometimes intentional).
- Colour: Must be printed on white or very light-coloured fabric only — sublimation inks are transparent and cannot override dark ground colours.
- Best for: Sportswear, athleisure, swimwear, activewear, leggings, performance apparel, all-over prints.
- Global market: ~$5.5 billion by 2025, growing 10%+ annually.

| Polyester Content | Vibrancy | Wash Durability | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 100% polyester | Excellent | 50+ cycles | Gold standard |
| 65%+ poly blend | Good | Moderate | Acceptable |
| 50/50 poly-cotton | Muted/vintage | Moderate | Stylistic only |
| <50% polyester | Poor | Low | Not recommended |
| 100% cotton | Fails | Washes out | Requires polymer coating |

**Canton Fair availability:** Strong — sublimation is one of the most prominent offerings. Leading exhibitors include Guangzhou Disen (sublimation printers/transfer paper) and heat transfer vinyl suppliers at the DPES Textile Printing Expo (held at the Canton Fair Complex).

---

### 1.4 Allover Print (AOP) vs. Placement Print

These are not separate techniques but describe *how the print is distributed across a garment* — a critical design and production decision.

#### Allover Print (AOP)
- The design covers the entire garment surface — front, back, sleeves, and across seams.
- Fabric is printed on the roll before cutting; the garment is then cut and sewn from printed yardage.
- You can tell AOP by checking seams and hems — if the print continues across them, it was AOP.
- Most commonly produced via sublimation (polyester) or rotary screen printing (any fabric).
- Artwork must be built as a **seamless repeat** — the tile must align on all four edges without visible joins.
- Best for: Bold fashion pieces, resort/swim, activewear, statement collections.

#### Placement Print (Engineered Print)
- Artwork is positioned precisely on a specific area of the finished garment — front chest, back yoke, sleeve panel, etc.
- Applied after cutting (and sometimes after sewing), via screen printing, DTG, or heat transfer.
- Artwork is stand-alone and sized correctly for the garment — it does not need to tile or repeat.
- Importantly: the placement is usually scaled to the garment's proportions at the design stage, so the visual impact is consistent across sizes.
- Best for: T-shirt graphics, logos, character artwork, slogan tees.

---

### 1.5 Heat Transfer Printing

A general category encompassing several sub-methods where the design is first produced on an intermediate carrier, then transferred to the garment with heat and pressure.

#### Heat Transfer Vinyl (HTV)
- Coloured vinyl film is cut to shape (plotter cutter), then heat pressed onto fabric.
- Finish options: Matte, gloss, glitter, metallic, neon, stretch, flock.
- Durable on cotton, polyester, and blends; good wash resistance.
- Best for: Simple shapes, names/numbers, single-colour or limited-colour logos.
- Limitation: Complex multi-colour designs require layering multiple cuts.

#### Heat Transfer Paper (Inkjet/Laser)
- Design is printed onto transfer paper, then pressed onto the garment.
- Cost-effective for short runs and photo-quality transfers.
- Durability: Lower than screen print or sublimation; can crack or peel if improperly applied.
- **Canton Fair availability:** Heat transfer paper and vinyl suppliers are prominent exhibitors (e.g., Visionsub at the 138th Canton Fair).

#### DTF (Direct-to-Film)
- Design is printed onto a special film, powder adhesive is applied, then the film is heat pressed onto any fabric.
- Key advantage over DTG: Works on cotton, polyester, denim, nylon, and blends without pre-treatment; works on dark fabrics.
- Produces vibrant, full-colour prints with good wash durability.
- Fast-growing: One of the most talked-about technologies at Canton Fair 2025.
- Best for: Small-to-medium runs needing fabric versatility.

---

### 1.6 Flocking, Foiling, and Metallic Effects

Specialty decorative effects applied on top of or in combination with base prints, typically using adhesive and a heat press.

#### Foiling
- Adhesive is screen-printed (or digitally applied) as the design shape. After curing, a metallic or holographic foil film is heat pressed onto the adhesive. Only the adhesive areas pick up the foil; the backing film is peeled away.
- Foil types: Gold, silver, bronze, holographic, iridescent, patterned.
- Result: Mirror-like reflective finish, high perceived luxury.
- Care: Wash inside out, cold water, no bleach or fabric softener — foil can crack if mishandled.
- Best for: Evening wear, festival fashion, logo highlights, limited-edition embellishment.

#### Flocking
- Same adhesive application as foiling, but instead of metal film, fine velvet-like fibres (flock) are bonded to the adhesive surface, often using an electrostatic charge to stand the fibres upright.
- Result: Tactile, velvet/suede texture.
- Pre-cut heat-press flock transfers are available for simpler applications.
- Best for: Premium branding, textured logos, heritage or country-club aesthetics.

#### Metallic and Puff Inks (Screen Print)
- Metallic inks contain reflective particles mixed into the ink base.
- Puff inks contain a blowing agent that expands under heat to create a raised, 3D feel.
- Both are applied as part of the screen printing process and are widely available at Canton Fair suppliers.

---

### 1.7 Resist-Dye Techniques

Techniques where dye is selectively prevented from reaching parts of the fabric, creating pattern through negative space.

#### Tie-Dye
- Fabric is folded, twisted, or crumpled into a shape and secured with rubber bands or string before immersion in dye.
- The bound areas resist dye penetration, creating characteristic swirl, spiral, or bullseye patterns.
- Known globally by different names: *bandhani* (India), *zha-ran* (China), *plangi* (Indonesia).
- Modern variations: Ice dye, fibre-reactive dye, acid dye for different fibres and colour effects.
- Production scale: Labour-intensive; each piece is unique. Best for artisanal or boutique runs.

#### Shibori
- Japanese resist-dye technique that predates modern tie-dye. Results are more controlled and geometric than Western tie-dye.
- Key methods:
  - **Kumo**: Fabric pleated and bound with thread — creates spiderweb or sunburst patterns.
  - **Arashi**: Fabric wrapped diagonally around a pole and compressed — creates diagonal stripe patterns.
  - **Itajime**: Fabric folded and clamped between carved wooden blocks — creates geometric shapes.
  - **Nui**: Areas are stitched before dyeing — allows the most precise pattern control.
- Traditionally done with indigo dye; increasingly done in a full colour range.
- Each piece is unique; highly prized in premium and artisan fashion.

#### Batik
- Wax (typically beeswax or paraffin) is applied to fabric in the pattern areas; the fabric is then dyed; the wax resists dye penetration; the wax is removed (by boiling or scraping), revealing the un-dyed pattern.
- Two main methods:
  - **Batik tulis** (hand-drawn): Artisan draws wax onto fabric with a *canting* tool — most labour-intensive, highest prestige.
  - **Batik cap** (stamped): Metal copper stamps (*cap*) imprint wax faster — more scalable, lower cost.
- Signature characteristic: "Ice crackling" — fine lines where wax cracked during handling, allowing dye to seep through. Considered a hallmark of authentic batik.
- Recognised by UNESCO as Intangible Cultural Heritage.
- **Canton Fair context:** Batik-style prints are available as machine-printed fabric (not hand-applied wax), produced by rotary screen or digital printing that mimics the batik aesthetic at scale.

## 2. Pattern / Motif Categories

What the print looks like — the visual vocabulary. Each category has sub-variants and carries distinct brand positioning implications.

---

### 2.1 Geometric

Patterns built from mathematical shapes, lines, and structures.

- **Stripes:** Vertical (lengthening effect), horizontal (widening effect), diagonal (dynamic). Awning stripes (bold, multicolour), pencil stripes (fine, formal), shadow stripes (tonal). Strong runway presence in 2025–26 (Jacquemus, Ralph Lauren).
- **Checks:** Gingham (equal-sized, 2-colour), buffalo check (large-scale gingham), windowpane (open grid), tattersall (small multicolour check). Classic, preppy, or country positioning.
- **Grids and graph paper:** Minimalist, utility-influenced.
- **Diamond / harlequin:** Rotated squares; traditional luxury or playful depending on colour palette.
- **Abstract geometric shapes:** Triangles, hexagons, irregular polygons, overlapping shapes. Strong in 2025–26 activewear and streetwear — especially in metallic shades creating optical illusions.
- **Optical / Op Art:** Geometric patterns engineered to create visual movement or 3D illusion (e.g., Vasarely-inspired). A recurring runway reference.

**Brand positioning:** Ranges from sporty/technical (bold geometric activewear) to classic/preppy (checks) to luxury (fine grid tailoring).

---

### 2.2 Florals

One of the most enduring and commercially powerful print categories.

- **Vintage botanical / William Morris-inspired:** Dense all-over arrangements, historically accurate flowers and foliage, rich earthy or jewel tones. Heritage and slow-fashion positioning.
- **Modern minimal florals:** Single stems, clean line-drawn flowers, lots of negative space. Contemporary, Scandi-influenced.
- **Bold tropical / maximalist:** Large-scale hibiscus, bird of paradise, monstera leaf. Resort wear staple; vibrant saturated colours.
- **Dark florals / Gothic botanical:** Flowers on black or deep navy/burgundy backgrounds; painterly brushstroke quality. Dominant in AW25/26 (Roberto Cavalli, McQueen, Anna Sui, Louis Vuitton). Strong commercial momentum.
- **Bedtime / ditsy florals:** Small-scale scattered blooms, soft palette; emerging for SS26.
- **Folkloric florals:** Embroidery-inspired, folk-art geometry, Eastern European or Mexican reference. Growing with heritage trend.

**Trend note (2025–26):** Dark florals are the strongest commercial variant right now — pairing black backgrounds with painterly or glitter-dusted flowers.

---

### 2.3 Animal Prints

One of the most consistent trend perennials; currently shifting toward abstraction.

- **Leopard:** Classic mark — irregular rosettes on tawny background. Never fully off-trend; currently being explored in non-traditional colourways (pink leopard, electric blue leopard).
- **Zebra:** High-contrast black-and-white organic stripes. Graphic and bold.
- **Snake / python:** Scale-like irregular tessellation. Often printed in tonal/neutral colourways; occasionally in brights.
- **Giraffe:** Large irregular patches, often in camel and white; rarer but recurring.
- **Cow print:** Black irregular patches on white; streetwear and contemporary market. Has had sustained commercial presence since early 2020s.
- **Abstract animal:** The key current shift — painterly interpretations of animal marks that read as modern art rather than literal skin. Tory Burch AW25 set the pace with speckled "Bambi-like" markings. SS26 continues with fluid abstract interpretations.
- **Camouflage (as animal-adjacent):** See section 2.8.

**Brand positioning:** Traditional leopard/zebra sits in accessible-luxury to fast-fashion; abstract animal positions in contemporary/designer.

---

### 2.4 Graphics / Text-Based

- **Slogan tees:** Single or multi-line text, often screen printed, often 1–2 colour. Requires clear spot-colour artwork.
- **Logos and wordmarks:** Brand identity repeated as pattern (monogram AOP) or placement chest hit.
- **Illustration / character art:** Hand-drawn figures, faces, editorial illustration style. Benefits from DTG or digital printing to preserve fine line quality.
- **Photo / photographic print:** Real photographic image printed onto fabric, usually via sublimation or digital AOP. Landscape, portrait, or fashion editorial styling.
- **Cartoon / pop art:** Bold outlines, flat fills, Ben-Day dots. Screen printing ideal.
- **Type-as-pattern:** Words or letters arranged to create an all-over repeat (e.g., repeated brand name in offset rows). Common in luxury streetwear.

**Canton Fair note:** Factories can produce graphic tees from vector files in very large quantities via rotary screen printing; for smaller runs with more colours, DTG or DTF is more economical.

---

### 2.5 Abstract / Painterly

- **Brushstroke / gestural:** Wide paintbrush marks, oil or watercolour textures. Often produced digitally from original artwork scans.
- **Watercolour wash:** Soft colour bleeds, irregular edges, atmospheric.
- **Inkblot / marble:** Organic fluid patterns formed by ink or dye in liquid media.
- **Gradient / ombre:** Gradual colour transition across the fabric — can be achieved through sublimation, dip-dye, or discharge. Very on-trend 2024–26.
- **Distorted / blurred:** Digitally manipulated images — the "blurred vision" trend identified for SS26 (Proenza Schouler) where patterns are treated as seen through frosted glass.
- **Collage / mixed media:** Layered torn paper textures, vintage ephemera, photographic fragments.

---

### 2.6 Conversational / Novelty Prints

Prints where the motifs are recognisable real-world objects arranged in a repeat or scattered layout.

- Examples: Food (pizza slices, cherries, lemons), transport (cars, planes), animals in non-traditional style (dogs in sunglasses), botanical specimens, space motifs (planets, stars), tools, vintage objects.
- Typically playful and accessible; often targeted at gifting, children's wear, or novelty adult apparel.
- Positioning: Fun/quirky brands; avoid for luxury or serious fashion positioning.
- **Production note:** Conversational prints often have complex fine-detail repeat arrangements — require high-quality digital artwork and are best produced via digital rotary or sublimation.

---

### 2.7 Heritage Prints

Prints rooted in specific weaving, regional, or historical textile traditions — produced as prints rather than woven constructions.

- **Tartan / plaid:** Grid of intersecting horizontal and vertical stripes forming a square repeat; associated with Scottish clan tradition. Many colourways (sett). Strong in tailoring and outerwear.
- **Houndstooth:** Abstract broken check formed of four-pointed shapes — classic menswear reference. Scale ranges from micro to macro.
- **Argyle:** Diamond shapes arranged in a diagonal grid with overlapping lines; historically knit-derived, now widely printed.
- **Paisley:** Teardrop-shaped curved motif with intricate internal pattern — Persian/Indian origin, adopted in Western fashion in the 1960s. Currently resurgent within the broader Baroque/maximalist trend.
- **Toile de Jouy:** Scenic pastoral engravings (typically figures in landscapes) printed in a single colour on white or cream. Currently being updated by designers with modern or satirical scenes.
- **Fair Isle:** Geometric snowflake and diamond patterns from Shetland knitting tradition; now also printed.
- **Ikat:** Blurred, irregular geometric pattern created by resist-dyeing yarns before weaving; can be printed to mimic this look at scale.
- **Damask:** Symmetrical floral or geometric motifs derived from woven jacquard — satin versus matte contrast. Common in evening wear and interiors-inspired fashion.

**Trend context:** Heritage prints are benefiting from the broader maximalism trend and the Baroque/Rococo wave on the AW25/26 runways.

---

### 2.8 Camouflage Variations

- **Military camo (US Woodland, MARPAT, Multicam):** Classic four-colour irregular shape pattern designed for terrain concealment. Strong streetwear and utility-wear association.
- **Urban / grey camo:** Same structure but in grey, black, and white tones for city context.
- **Fashion camo:** Camo pattern recoloured in non-military palettes (pink, purple, yellow) — keeps the aesthetic but subverts the military read.
- **Abstract camo:** Camo-inspired organic shapes deconstructed into painterly or geometric form.
- **Digital camo:** Pixelated camo — inspired by US Army's ACUPAT pattern; strongly associated with early 2000s streetwear; currently in vintage revival.

---

### 2.9 Tie-Dye / Marbled / Gradient Effects

Prints that simulate or are produced by fluid dye, resist, or digital distortion techniques.

- **Tie-dye (printed):** Digitally rendered or photographically-derived tie-dye pattern printed at scale — looks like hand-made but is repeatable. Usually sublimation or digital rotary.
- **Shibori (printed):** Indigo and white abstract geometric marks printed to mimic traditional Japanese binding techniques.
- **Marble:** Fluid swirling ink pattern; typically grey-and-white (stone reference) or multicolour.
- **Ombre / gradient:** Smooth colour fade from one hue to another — across the fabric length or width.
- **Batik (printed):** Crackled wax-resist look printed digitally or via rotary — achieves the characteristic ice-crackle texture at scale without hand-applied wax.
- **Acid wash effect:** Print simulating the bleached-out look of acid-washed denim.

## 3. Print Placement Terminology

Precise placement language is required in tech packs and briefs to avoid misinterpretation by factories.

---

### 3.1 Standard Placement Zones

| Term | Description |
|---|---|
| **Front chest (FC)** | Centred or off-centre on the upper front of the garment, roughly at chest height. The most common T-shirt placement. |
| **Left chest (LC)** | Small print on the left chest, typically logo-sized (70–100mm wide). Polo shirt standard. |
| **Centre front (CF)** | Large print running down the vertical centre of the front panel. |
| **Full front** | Print spanning the entire front panel from shoulder to hem. |
| **Back (CB / back yoke)** | Centred on the back panel. Can be full back, upper back/yoke, or lower back. |
| **Sleeve** | Upper arm, lower arm, or full sleeve. Can be single sleeve or both. Requires registration precision. |
| **Collar / neckline** | Print at or near the neckline; sometimes inside the collar. |
| **Hem** | Lower edge of the garment — often a stripe or tonal band. |
| **Side seam** | Print running along the side seam panel; common in sportswear. |
| **Allover (AOP)** | Print covers the entire garment surface — front, back, sleeves, and across seams. |

---

### 3.2 Centred vs. Off-Centre

- **Centred:** Artwork is placed on the exact horizontal centreline of the garment. The most neutral, broadly wearable choice.
- **Off-centre / asymmetric:** Artwork is deliberately shifted left, right, or dropped lower. Creates a more fashion-forward, contemporary feel. Requires precise measurement in the tech pack to replicate consistently across production.

---

### 3.3 Repeat Patterns vs. Engineered Prints

| | Repeat Pattern | Engineered Print |
|---|---|---|
| **Definition** | Motif tiles seamlessly across the full fabric width and length. | Artwork is positioned once, sized to fit the specific garment. |
| **When printed** | Before cutting — on the fabric roll. | After cutting, or on the finished garment. |
| **Artwork type** | Must be a seamless tile — all four edges must align. | Stand-alone artwork; no repeat required. |
| **Variation by size** | Pattern placement will vary slightly by garment size (a feature, not a bug). | Placement is proportionally consistent across sizes. |
| **Typical use** | Floralwall prints, heritage prints, AOP fashion fabric. | Graphic tees, logo placements, character art. |
| **Key skill needed** | Textile repeat design (specialist skill). | Standard illustration/graphic design. |

**Note for briefs:** When requesting a repeat pattern from a factory or print studio, always specify: tile width × height (in mm or cm), repeat type (straight/brick/half-drop/mirror), and a minimum colour count.

## 4. Colour Count and Complexity

Understanding how colour affects cost and process selection is essential for briefing manufacturers accurately.

---

### 4.1 How Colour Count Affects Screen Printing Cost

Each colour in a screen print requires a separate screen. Screens have setup costs (film, coating, exposure, cleaning) and physical space on the press.

- **1 colour:** Cheapest to set up; often used for slogan tees, minimalist logos.
- **2–4 colours (spot colour):** The sweet spot for screen printing — cost-effective, vibrant, reliable.
- **5–8 colours:** Increases setup cost; at this point simulated process may become more economical than true spot colour.
- **9+ colours / full photographic range:** Better handled by digital printing (DTG/DTF/sublimation) unless order volume justifies rotary screen setup.

Rule of thumb: **Every additional colour in a screen print adds screen setup cost** — typically USD $15–50 per screen per colour, paid once per design. This is amortised across the run, so large orders spread the cost more efficiently.

---

### 4.2 Spot Colour vs. CMYK vs. Simulated Process

#### Spot Colour
- Each colour is a **premixed ink** matched to a Pantone (PMS) reference.
- Highest colour accuracy and consistency across the run.
- No halftones — solid blocks of colour only (unless halftones are intentionally added for shading).
- Best for: Logos, bold graphics, designs requiring exact brand colour matching.
- Cost efficient for 1–4 colours.

#### CMYK (Full Colour Process)
- Uses four transparent inks — Cyan, Magenta, Yellow, Key (Black) — printed as overlapping halftone dots to simulate the full colour spectrum.
- Only **four screens** required regardless of design complexity.
- Works best on **white or very light garments** — inks are transparent, so dark fabrics show through and muddy the colours.
- Cannot match Pantone references precisely.
- Best for: Photo-style prints on white garments.

#### Simulated Process
- Combines spot colours and halftones to reproduce complex multi-colour designs on any fabric colour.
- Typically uses 8–12 screens.
- More stable during production than CMYK on dark garments; preferred for T-shirt graphic prints that need to work on black or navy.
- Best reserved for high-volume runs where setup cost is justified.

| Method | Screens | Works on Dark? | Colour Accuracy | Best Use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Spot colour | 1 per colour | Yes | Exact PMS | Logos, 1–4 colour graphics |
| CMYK | 4 | No | Approximate | Photo prints on white |
| Simulated process | 8–12 | Yes | High | Complex multi-colour; dark garments |

---

### 4.3 Photographic vs. Vector Artwork Requirements

**Vector artwork** (Illustrator .ai, .eps, .svg):
- Built from mathematical paths, not pixels — scales to any size without quality loss.
- Required for spot colour screen printing — each colour must be a separate, clean layer.
- Fonts must be outlined (converted to shapes) before sending to factory.
- Best for: Logos, text-based designs, flat illustration, geometric patterns.

**Photographic / raster artwork** (Photoshop .psd, .tiff, high-res .png):
- Made of pixels — resolution is fixed at creation.
- Minimum 300 DPI at actual print size.
- Required for designs with photographic detail, textures, or painted/drawn elements.
- Best for: DTG, sublimation, DTF, and digital rotary screen printing.
- CMYK colour mode for print output (not RGB, which is for screens).

## 5. File Format and Artwork Requirements for Sourcing

When sending print artwork to Canton Fair or other Chinese manufacturing partners, the file requirements below should be specified in the tech pack or email brief.

---

### 5.1 What Factories Need

**Gold standard: Vector file**
- `.ai` (Adobe Illustrator) — most widely accepted
- `.eps` — universally compatible
- `.pdf` (with embedded vectors, not flattened)
- `.svg` — acceptable for digital printing workflows

All fonts outlined (converted to curves/paths). All colours labelled with Pantone PMS codes (for screen printing) or noted as CMYK values.

**For photographic / all-over prints:**
- `.psd` or `.tiff` — layered if possible
- `.png` with transparent background at 300 DPI minimum at final print dimensions
- Colour mode: **CMYK** (not RGB)
- Actual size: Provide the file at the real-world print dimensions (e.g., a 30cm × 40cm chest print should be a 30 × 40cm file at 300 DPI — 3,543 × 4,724 pixels)

**Repeat patterns for AOP / fabric printing:**
- Supply the repeat tile + indicate tile dimensions (e.g., "25cm × 30cm half-drop repeat")
- Confirm whether the tile should be applied horizontally, as a half-drop, or brick repeat
- Include a scaled mockup showing how the repeat looks at garment scale

---

### 5.2 Colour Specification

- **Screen printing:** Always specify Pantone Coated (C) references for each colour. Note: "Pantone 485 C = Red" not "red."
- **Sublimation / DTG / DTF:** Provide CMYK or RGB hex values; understand that screen-to-fabric colour shift occurs — request a colour proof or pre-production sample.
- Include a physical Pantone fan deck chip in sample packages to manufacturing partners where possible.

---

### 5.3 Common Artwork Mistakes That Delay Production

| Mistake | Why It Causes Delay | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Low-resolution raster files (below 300 DPI) | Print looks pixelated; factory must request new file or redraw | Always provide 300 DPI at actual print size |
| RGB colour mode instead of CMYK | Colours shift unpredictably when printed | Convert to CMYK in Photoshop/Illustrator before sending |
| Fonts not outlined in vector files | Factory computer may not have the same font; text reflows | Outline all fonts before exporting |
| Upsampled files (resolution artificially increased) | Appears 300 DPI but still pixelated — factory cannot use | Never upscale; always start from original high-res source |
| No placement dimensions specified | Factory guesses print size and position; first sample is wrong | Include dimensions and distance from seam/collar in mm |
| Screenshots or social media crops sent as artwork | Too low resolution and often has compression artefacts | Always provide original artwork files |
| No colour references (just colour names) | "Blue" means different things to different people | Specify Pantone C codes or CMYK values |
| Last-minute design changes after sample approval | Factory halts production to reprint; lead time extends | Freeze artwork before approving pre-production sample |
| Repeat tile edges not aligned | Visible seam lines appear across fabric in production | Test repeat by tiling the file in Illustrator/Photoshop before sending |
| Missing bleed for allover prints | White or raw fabric visible at seam edges | Add 5–10mm bleed beyond the garment edge |

## 6. Pattern Scale and Proportion

Scale — how large or small the motif appears relative to the garment and the human body — is one of the most powerful design decisions in print.

---

### 6.1 Micro Patterns

- Motifs are small relative to the garment — the design may read as a texture or tone from a normal viewing distance, revealing its pattern only up close.
- Examples: Micro-houndstooth, ditsy florals, tiny stripes, micro geometric grids.
- Effect on the garment: The silhouette and cut remain dominant — the fabric reads as a textured solid. Good for tailoring and suiting fabrics where the garment shape matters more than the print.
- Body proportion effect: Tends to be more universally flattering — does not add visual weight.
- Production note: Micro patterns require high print resolution — rotary screen printing may struggle with very fine detail; digital printing or fine-mesh screen printing preferred.

---

### 6.2 Macro Patterns

- Motifs are large relative to the garment — the print becomes the dominant visual element; silhouette reads secondary.
- Examples: Large-scale tropical florals, oversized houndstooth, bold graphic placement prints, large leopard spots.
- Effect on the garment: The print commands attention; cut is secondary. Can make garments appear larger on the body.
- Body proportion effect: Large-scale prints can overpower a petite frame; they work best on bolder or longer silhouettes.
- Production note: Large motifs must be carefully engineered for repeat — irregular repeat tiles or printed-to-garment (engineered) placement to avoid the motif being cut awkwardly at seams.
- Trend context: Macro scale is strongly aligned with the current maximalism wave.

---

### 6.3 Scale in Relation to Silhouette

- A full, voluminous silhouette (wide trousers, oversized jacket, maxi skirt) can carry a large macro print — the print and the shape work together in scale.
- A fitted, minimal silhouette benefits from micro or medium-scale prints — the cut remains the hero.
- A bold macro print on a fitted garment creates high visual impact; a micro print on a voluminous silhouette reads as quiet texture.
- **Proportion principle:** Design elements — including print scale — should be proportional to the total garment and the wearer's body. Patterns that dominate the eye should be reserved for garments with enough fabric surface to display them fully.

---

### 6.4 Scale and Repeat Tile Sizing

When specifying a repeat pattern for fabric printing, the tile size directly determines the motif scale on the garment:

- A tile of 10 × 10cm will produce a noticeably different scale than 25 × 25cm even with the same motif.
- Standard commercial fabric repeat widths: 10–20cm for smaller-scale prints; 25–50cm for large-scale fashion prints.
- For engineered/placement prints: the artwork is sized to the garment at the sample stage and the size is locked — it does not change with garment size (the placement simply shifts slightly as the garment gets larger/smaller).

## 7. Trend Context for Prints (2025–2026)

Current commercial direction — what is selling, what is gaining momentum, and why.

---

### 7.1 The Overarching Shift: Maximalism

The most significant macro-trend shaping print direction in 2025–26 is the return of maximalism — a broad cultural shift away from the minimalist neutrals that dominated the 2018–2022 period. Social media dynamics (photogenic, statement-making outfits gaining algorithmic traction) and post-pandemic desire for self-expression are accelerating this shift.

For brands: this is the moment to be bolder with prints. Quiet luxury still has its audience, but the commercial growth is in expressive print.

---

### 7.2 Strongest Print Trends Right Now

**Dark Florals**
The single most commercially powerful print direction in AW25/26. Black or very deep-ground florals with painterly or photographic flower rendering. Seen at Roberto Cavalli, McQueen, Anna Sui, Louis Vuitton. Growing into SS26. High commercial adoption expected in accessible and mid-market.

**Abstract Animal Prints**
Moving beyond literal leopard and zebra into fluid, painterly interpretations of animal markings. Tory Burch AW25 set the direction with speckled, Bambi-like marks. The print category remains dominant but the execution is evolving toward fine-art reference.

**Baroque and Heritage Patterns**
Paisleys, damasks, ornate symmetrical florals, guild-inspired motifs. Driven by the broader Baroque/Rococo wave (Etro, Dior, Burberry, Alice + Olivia). Maximalist but rooted in craft tradition — appeals to consumers who find tie-dye or graphic prints too casual.

**Geometric / Op Art**
Bold geometric shapes in metallic tones, especially layered to create optical depth. Futuristic and tech-adjacent positioning. Strong in activewear and streetwear; increasingly crossing into mainline fashion.

**Stripes (Updated)**
Awning stripes in South of France palette (Jacquemus); refined cabana stripes; wide-format irregular stripes. Stripes are perennially commercial — the current iteration is more oversized and colour-saturated than the preceding minimalist stripe era.

**Polka Dots (Refined)**
Having a luxury moment — from Jacquemus's Paris return to Marc Jacobs. Ranging from very large-scale graphic dots to delicate micro-dots in sophisticated colourways. Not novelty; genuinely elevated.

**Retro / Psychedelic / 70s**
Swirling geometrics, paisley, psychedelic colour clashes (mustard, burnt orange, deep purple). Pattern-clashing encouraged. Strong in festival and occasion wear.

**Blurred Vision / Digitally Distorted**
An emerging SS26 direction — simple patterns digitally manipulated to appear seen through glass, water, or frosted surfaces. Proenza Schouler led this. Requires digital printing (sublimation or digital rotary) to execute accurately.

---

### 7.3 Patterns with Sustained Commercial Longevity

These print families perform reliably across trend cycles and are always safe bets for new brands:

- Classic leopard (especially in non-standard colourways)
- Gingham and check (perennial for casual and summer collections)
- Stripes of any width
- Tie-dye and gradient effects (especially in elevated executions)
- Simple florals with a modern colour update

---

### 7.4 What's Coming: 2026 and Beyond

- **AI-generated and AI-distorted prints:** Patterns created or manipulated by generative AI tools; aesthetics that reference computational or algorithmic origin.
- **Interactive / responsive prints:** Heat-sensitive, UV-reactive, or photochromic inks that change colour — early-adopter territory, increasingly available from specialist suppliers.
- **Heritage craft revival:** Batik, shibori, ikat, and resist-dye aesthetics continuing to grow as global craft references become more mainstream in Western fashion.
- **Personalised/customised AOP:** As DTF and DTG production costs fall, custom one-piece or small-run AOP becomes more accessible for emerging brands.

## Quick Reference: Print Decision Matrix

| Scenario | Recommended Technique | Key Requirements |
|---|---|---|
| Bold graphic tee, 500+ units | Screen printing (spot colour, 1–4 colours) | Vector .ai file, Pantone refs |
| Full-colour photograph on white tee | CMYK screen or DTG | 300 DPI TIFF/PNG, CMYK mode |
| All-over print activewear, polyester | Sublimation AOP | Seamless repeat tile, CMYK |
| Small run (<100 pieces), complex artwork | DTG or DTF | 300 DPI PNG, actual print size |
| Vintage/retro soft hand feel | Discharge screen print | 100% cotton garment, vector artwork |
| Metallic or holographic logo highlight | Foil heat transfer | Vector shape, Pantone ref |
| Velvet/tactile effect on logo | Flocking | Vector shape |
| Artisanal small-run fashion pieces | Tie-dye / shibori (genuine) | Accept variation; each piece unique |
| Heritage fabric aesthetic at scale | Rotary screen, batik-style | Repeat tile, 6–12 colour max |
| Fast sampling, all fabric types | DTF | 300 DPI PNG at actual size |
